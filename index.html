Pecos Reservoirs · Management & Reuse Lab

Companion to the Salinity Lab Real data 2000–2020 SWAT+ gwflow · Pecos_USA

Interactive tool for thinking through where reuse water (treated oilfield produced water, or recycled municipal water) could go along the Pecos River, using the real management history of the 5 major dams — Santa Rosa, Sumner, Brantley, Avalon, and Red Bluff — between New Mexico and Texas. In Flow & Management you can test your own release policy for any dam (or try a one-click scenario) and watch the river — drawn as a single connected line through all 5 dams — light up or go dark; in Where to Place Reuse Water that same river diagram turns green/yellow/red so you can click a stretch directly. Once the SWAT+/gwflow model of the Pecos is finished, the simplified "dilution capacity" indicator here will be replaced by reach-by-reach simulated salt transport from the Salinity Lab, closing the loop between reservoir management and optimal reuse siting.

5. Or adjust each dam by hand

What these controls mean:
  • Custom rule — turn this on for a dam to test a different way it could be operated, instead of exactly what happened historically. Leave it off to keep using the real record for that dam.
  • Scale — how much more or less water that dam releases each month, as a percent of what it actually released historically. 100% = no change. 150% = one-and-a-half times as much every month. 50% = half as much.
  • Min. floor — a guaranteed minimum amount of water that dam must release every month, no matter how dry it gets — even in months when it historically released almost nothing. This works like a minimum-instream-flow rule that protects the river downstream.

Turning on the rule for one dam only changes the river reach right below that dam (and everything further downstream, until the next dam re-regulates the water). Turning on several dams at once lets you test a whole basin-wide policy — for example, guaranteeing a minimum flow everywhere.

How often would this reach be risky? (full 2000–2020 record)

Instead of looking at just one month, this uses the full 252 months of real data for the chosen reach to count how often your proposed volume would exceed different flow thresholds — i.e., how often this site would have had little dilution capacity. The chart below always reflects your current rule settings (historical if you haven't changed anything).
How to read this: a "good" reuse site is one where the % of months in the red zone is low — a reach with high, stable flow most of the year. A reach that turns red often (especially in 2011–2013, or in summer) needs a smaller reuse volume, additional treatment, relocation downstream of a dam with steadier releases, or a minimum-release rule on the upstream dam — try setting a floor on the Flow & Management tab and watch these numbers change.
Exactly how is this computed?

For each of the 252 months with valid data: ratio = (reuse_volume/12) / dam_release_that_month. The dam release is the observed historical value, or your custom rule's output for that dam if you've enabled one — either way it's the release of the dam immediately upstream of that reach (for the reach below Red Bluff, Red Bluff's own release/rule is used as a lower bound — there are unmeasured additional inflows further downstream, so the real risk there is likely lower than shown).

Green = ratio < 2% (high dilution). Yellow = 2–10% (moderate). Red = >10%, or flow ≈ 0 (minimal or no dilution).

This is a water-quantity proxy, not a salt-transport model — it does not account for background concentration, mixing time, or river–aquifer exchange. That step belongs to the Salinity Lab (SWAT+/gwflow).

The Pecos is already a salty river — before adding anything

Much of the Pecos basin in New Mexico and Texas crosses Permian evaporite formations (Rustler and Salado): salt and gypsum that groundwater dissolves as it circulates. Where that saline water surfaces or connects with the river — notably at Malaga Bend, about 18 miles southeast of Carlsbad, NM — the river receives a documented natural brine load far above background.

Documented brine source — Malaga Bend. USGS studies attribute the salinity here to brine leaking upward from a confined aquifer at the base of the Rustler Formation, mixing with fresher shallow groundwater and discharging to the Pecos at roughly 0.5 cubic feet per second of saturated brine in this reach — enough to measurably raise river TDS. USGS field-tested a brine-diversion approach here decades ago under the Malaga Bend Experimental Salinity Alleviation Project, one of the earliest documented attempts to intervene in the Pecos's natural salt budget.
Fish don't see it the same everywhere. The Pecos pupfish (Cyprinodon pecosensis) — a fish found only in the Pecos drainage — currently persists in the upper basin, from Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge (near Roswell, NM) down to just north of Brantley Reservoir. It has been eliminated from the middle and lower Pecos, including the reach through Malaga Bend and Red Bluff, where high natural salinity is one of the documented pressures. In November 2024, the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service proposed listing it as Threatened under the Endangered Species Act, with critical habitat covering about 136 river miles and 26,555 acres across New Mexico and a Texas population in Salt Creek — not yet finalized as of this writing.

This tab is informational: exact brine flow rates, remediation outcomes, and the final ESA listing/critical-habitat status should be verified against the official sources below before being used in a report or decision — the point here is that whoever plays with this map knows the salinity problem, and the fish response to it, are real and already documented.

Why this doesn't compute salt (yet)

This lab uses real flow (Mm³/month released by each dam) as a proxy for dilution capacity. It doesn't move TDS, doesn't know whether a reach is gaining or losing groundwater, and doesn't know the background salt load in each reach.

That's exactly what the Salinity Lab (gwflow + SWAT+) does: advection–dispersion transport of TDS, river–aquifer exchange via streambed conductance, and comparison against EPA/agricultural/livestock thresholds.

The integration plan: once the SWAT+/gwflow model of the Pecos (346×227 cells, 2 km) is calibrated using these same observed releases as its flow forcing, every reach between dams will have a simulated TDS concentration. That number replaces the "% of flow" indicator in the previous tab with a real result: how much reuse water a reach can actually take without exceeding the TDS threshold that affects the Pecos pupfish or downstream uses.

How the data was aggregated for this lab

  • The daily CSVs (~7,671 days × 5 dams) were aggregated to monthly totals/averages (252 months) to keep the file light and easy to explore — the full daily detail is still available in gwflow/input_files/obs_reservoir/ for finer analysis.
  • Monthly release is the sum of that month's daily m³, converted to Mm³. Monthly storage is the mean of that month's observed acre-feet (af).
  • "% of storage" in the Management tab uses each dam's own 2000–2020 observed maximum as the reference — not its certified design capacity, which is now shown separately above.
  • "Reach flow" in the Reuse tab uses the release of the dam immediately upstream of that reach as a proxy — it does not include unmeasured tributary inflow or irrigation return flow between dams, so it underestimates real available flow over long reaches.

How the "what-if" release rules and scenarios work

  • The rule editor (Flow & Management tab) is not a hydrologic model — it's a simple policy calculator layered on the real observed record: your_release = max(floor, historical_release × scale), applied month by month, per dam.
  • It does not simulate reservoir storage, inflow, evaporation, or downstream re-regulation — a rule you set on one dam changes the flow shown in the reach immediately below it, but the tool does not attempt to model what the next dam downstream would then do with that extra or reduced water.
  • The 5 preset scenarios (buttons above the table) are illustrative starting points, not forecasts or recommendations — each just sets specific scale/floor values, described in plain language when you click it.
  • The river diagram's flow-mode colors use a fixed 0–30 Mm³/month scale for visual contrast; the risk-mode colors use the same low/moderate/high thresholds documented in the "Where to Place Reuse Water" tab's "Exactly how is this computed?" panel.
